# Razor Bumps and Ingrown Hairs: Causes, Prevention and Fixes

Razor bumps are one of the most common complaints barbers hear, and one of the most misunderstood. Most men assume they have sensitive skin and buy a new aftershave. Usually the problem isn't the skin. It's the way the hair is being cut.

The good news is that most razor bumps are preventable with changes to technique and tools rather than expensive products. The less good news is that some men — particularly those with tightly curled hair — are prone to them no matter how carefully they shave, and for them the answer may be to stop shaving so close.

A quick disclaimer: we're barbers, not doctors. What follows is well-established general guidance. Persistent, painful or infected bumps are a job for a family doctor or dermatologist.

## What Razor Bumps Actually Are

Razor bumps, known medically as **pseudofolliculitis barbae**, happen when a shaved hair grows back into the skin instead of out of it. The body treats the trapped hair as a foreign object, and the result is a red, sometimes painful bump — occasionally with a visible hair underneath or a small pus-filled head.

There are two main ways this happens:

- **The hair curls back** after being cut and re-enters the skin from the surface.
- **The hair is cut below the skin line** and grows sideways into the follicle wall before it ever emerges.

Pseudofolliculitis barbae is especially common in men with tightly curled hair, because the hair naturally curves back toward the skin as it grows. It can affect anyone who shaves closely, though.

**Razor burn** is different: it's surface irritation — redness, stinging, a rash-like look — that shows up within minutes or hours of shaving, usually from friction, dull blades or poor prep. It often happens alongside bumps but has a slightly different cause.

## Why Multi-Blade Cartridges Make It Worse

Multi-blade razors are designed for closeness. The first blade catches and slightly lifts the hair, and the following blades cut it — sometimes just below the skin surface, after it has retracted.

That's what makes cartridges feel so smooth. It's also exactly the mechanism that lets hair grow back under the skin. For men who don't get bumps, it's not a problem. For men who do, more blades often means more bumps.

## Shaving Against the Grain

Shaving against the direction of growth gives the closest result and causes the most trouble. It cuts hair shorter, pulls on the follicle and puts more blade passes on the skin.

Find your grain by running a hand over two or three days of stubble. Smooth means with the grain; rough means against. Many men have different directions on the neck than on the cheeks — the neck often grows in swirls, which is why it's where bumps are most common.

If you get bumps: shave with the grain only. You'll lose a little closeness. You'll also lose most of the irritation.

## Prep Is Most of the Work

The difference between a shave that leaves bumps and one that doesn't is often decided before the razor touches your face.

1. **Shave after a shower, or after a hot, wet towel.** Warm water softens the hair dramatically, so it cuts with less force and less pulling.
2. **Wash first.** Oil and dead skin get dragged along by the blade.
3. **Use a proper lather.** A shaving cream or soap worked in with a brush lifts the hairs away from the skin. Canned foam sits on top and does less.
4. **Let it sit for a minute** before shaving.
5. **Use a sharp blade.** Dull blades tug, and tugging leads to irritation.
6. **Light pressure, short strokes, rinse often.**
7. **Finish with cool water and an alcohol-free moisturizer or balm.**

## Single Blade vs Cartridge vs Trimmer

| Tool | Closeness | Bump risk | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Multi-blade cartridge** | Very close | Highest for bump-prone skin | Men who don't get bumps |
| **Single-blade safety razor** | Close | Lower | Men who want a clean shave with less irritation |
| **Electric foil shaver** | Moderate | Lower | Quick daily shaves |
| **Trimmer with short guard** | Stubble (roughly 1mm) | Lowest | Men with persistent bumps or tightly curled hair |

For men with a strong tendency to bumps, the most reliable fix is often the simplest: **stop shaving to bare skin.** Leaving a millimetre or so of stubble means the hair tip stays above the surface and can't curl back in. Many dermatologists suggest this for pseudofolliculitis barbae. A short, clean stubble with sharp lines can look every bit as tidy as a full shave.

## Exfoliation and Aftercare

Dead skin can trap hair at the surface, so gentle, regular exfoliation helps.

- **Exfoliate two or three times a week**, not daily. A soft brush, washcloth or mild scrub is enough.
- **Products with salicylic acid or glycolic acid** are commonly used to keep follicles clear. Start slowly; both can irritate if overused.
- **Don't exfoliate right before or right after shaving.** Give the skin a day.
- **Moisturize daily.** Dry skin is more easily irritated.

## What Not to Do

- **Don't pick or squeeze bumps.** It pushes bacteria deeper, increases the risk of infection, and is a common cause of dark marks and scarring — especially on darker skin.
- **Don't dig out ingrown hairs with tweezers.** If a hair is clearly looped above the surface, it can be gently freed with a clean tool. If it's under the skin, leave it.
- **Don't shave over active bumps.** Give the area a few days off. Shaving over them reopens the problem.
- **Don't keep using the same blade for weeks.**
- **Don't splash on high-alcohol aftershave.** It stings and dries, and dry skin irritates more easily.

## When to See a Doctor

See your family doctor or a dermatologist if:

- Bumps are painful, swollen, hot or filled with pus.
- They keep coming back despite changes to your routine.
- You're developing dark marks, raised scars or keloids.
- The problem is spreading or getting worse.

There are prescription treatments for persistent cases, and a doctor can rule out other conditions that look similar, such as folliculitis caused by bacteria or fungus. That's medicine, and it isn't something a barber or an article should diagnose.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long do razor bumps take to go away?**
Mild bumps often settle within a few days to a couple of weeks if you stop shaving the area. Persistent or recurring bumps are worth raising with a doctor.

**Are razor bumps the same as acne?**
No, though they can look similar. Razor bumps are caused by hairs growing back into the skin and appear where you shave.

**Does growing a beard get rid of razor bumps?**
For many men, yes — once the hair is long enough, it can't curve back into the skin. Even a short stubble length helps.

**Why do I get bumps mostly on my neck?**
Neck hair often grows in several directions and lies flatter to the skin, which makes it easier to shave against the grain without realizing it.

**Should I use a new razor every time?**
Not every time, but replace blades regularly. A dull blade pulls rather than cuts.
